The sample is the only point in the whole process where you hold the product before several hundred of them exist. Everything before it is a drawing. Everything after it is a repeat of what you approved.

Most buyers treat the sample as a formality — a box to tick before the real order. The ones who get the best results treat it as the last cheap opportunity to change their mind. This guide covers what drives the timeline, what a sample fee actually means, and what to inspect on each type of bag.

## Why 7 Days for One Sample and 15 for Another

**Sampling runs 7–15 days.** Where your project lands in that window depends on what you are asking us to make.

| Faster end of the window | Longer end of the window |
|---|---|
| An existing style with a colour change | A brand-new pattern built from scratch |
| Standard fabric already in stock | Special or non-standard fabric to source |
| Simple construction — tote, pouch, cosmetic bag | Complex construction — backpack, multi-compartment styles |
| Few components | Many zippers, buckles, adjusters, mesh panels |
| Standard colours | Pantone matching that needs dye trials |
| Branding already used before | Moulded branding requiring tooling |

**The pattern is the long pole.** For a style we have never built, the pattern has to be drafted, cut and tested before a single bag exists. For a style you are adjusting, that work is already done.

**Tell us your deadline at the start.** If you have a fixed date, say so before sampling begins rather than after. Sometimes we can prioritise; sometimes we will tell you honestly that the specification needs simplifying to make the date.

## What to Check When It Arrives

Do these four things before anything else. They catch most of what goes wrong.

1. **Check it loaded, not empty.** Put in what your buyer will actually carry. An empty bag tells you the shape; a loaded bag tells you whether the size works.
2. **Check colour in daylight.** Not under office lighting, not on a screen. If you have a Pantone reference, hold it against the sample.
3. **Check the branding against what you approved.** Position, size and method — all three, not just that the logo is present.
4. **Open and close every zipper ten times.** Zippers are the most common failure point on any bag, and problems show up in the first few cycles rather than later.

## What to Check, by Category

Different bags fail in different places. This is where the sample earns its cost.

| Category | Check these first |
|---|---|
| Backpack | Shoulder strap width and padding; back panel shape against a real back; laptop sleeve with the actual device; base reinforcement under load |
| Waist bag | Belt length at both extremes of your buyer range; buckle strength and one-handed release; whether it stays put when you move |
| Crossbody / chest bag | Strap adjustment range and whether it holds position; how it sits when walking; opening access with one hand |
| Tote | Handle attachment under a heavy load — this is the failure point; base structure; whether it stands or collapses |
| Cosmetic bag | Zipper smoothness at this small size; lining quality; whether it stands upright with contents |
| Duffel | Strap anchor points; base reinforcement; whether it holds shape when partly full |

## Giving Feedback That Saves You a Round

Each round of corrections means another sample and another cycle. The single biggest cause of a long sampling stage is feedback arriving in pieces.

- **Send everything at once.** Photograph what is wrong, mark it up, and send one consolidated list rather than three messages over a week.
- **Say what you want, not only what is wrong.** "The strap is too short" is half the information. "The strap needs another 10 cm of adjustment range" is actionable.
- **Separate must-change from nice-to-have.** We will tell you which nice-to-haves are free and which cost you a cycle.
- **Tell us if something is right.** Knowing what to leave alone is as useful as knowing what to fix.

**Then approve in writing.** Bulk is produced against the approved sample, so what you sign off is what gets made 500 times.

## FAQ

**How long does sampling take?**
Sampling runs 7–15 days. Simple constructions in standard fabrics with an existing pattern sit at the faster end. A brand-new pattern, special fabric sourcing, Pantone matching or complex multi-component construction sits at the longer end. Tell us your deadline before sampling starts rather than after.

**Is the sample free?**
Sample fees are refundable on orders of 1,000 pieces and above, and negotiable between 500 and 1,000. Below that, discuss it with Anna before sampling starts. A sample requires a pattern, materials and a skilled person making one bag by hand, which is why it carries a fee that returns when the order justifies it.

**What should I check when the sample arrives?**
Check it loaded rather than empty, check colour in daylight against your Pantone reference, check branding position and size against what you approved, and cycle every zipper several times. Then check the details specific to your category — strap anchors on a tote, belt length on a waist bag, laptop fit on a backpack.

**How many rounds of samples are normal?**
One round is common for a straightforward adjustment to an existing style. Two is normal for a new pattern. More than that usually means feedback arrived in pieces rather than all at once, or that the specification changed during sampling.

**Can I order bulk without a sample?**
You can, but we do not recommend it. Bulk is produced against an approved reference, and without a physical sample that reference is a drawing. The sample is the only stage where a change costs you days rather than a full production run.

**What happens if the bulk does not match the sample?**
Bulk is produced against the sample you approved, which is why written approval matters. Keep your approved sample — it is the reference for both sides. Raise any discrepancy with Anna with photographs as soon as you receive the goods.

**Can I ask for a sample in a different colour to my order?**
Yes, but confirm colour on a sample in the actual production colour before bulk. Fabric colour behaves differently across weaves and coatings, and a colour approved on one fabric is not a reliable guide to another.
